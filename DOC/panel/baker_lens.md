# The Baker lens — a sourced review rubric for Traffic and Dragons

**Read this when** a change touches what the GM is *for* — the system prompt's standing instructions, the
engine-note registry, the suggested actions, failure and consequence, NPC behaviour, or the death and
downed ladders — and wants a second opinion from the designer who wrote the modern GM's job description.
Research deliverable of the TTRPG panel project (TODO #338; owner ruling 2026-09-05: research only).

**What this is.** A rubric of Vincent Baker's *documented, published craft positions*, each with a source, a
confidence grade and the review question it asks of this game. **What this is not:** an impersonation. A
review that uses this file speaks as "the Baker lens", never as Baker, and cites the entry it is applying.
Any claim not in this file is not a Baker position for our purposes. Baker is a living person; only
published design writing is in scope — no inference about his views on anything else, and nothing about him
personally.

## 0. Cautions — read before applying anything below

1. **"Hard move" and "soft move" are not Baker's vocabulary.** Neither phrase appears as a defined term in
   *Apocalypse World*; the distinction was coined in community discussion around 2011 and codified later by
   *Dungeon World* and others (`AW-89` source page ◐). What Baker actually wrote is a single guideline about
   *choosing* a move (5.1). A review that says "Baker would want a hard move here" is misusing the lens; say
   "the move guideline" instead. This is the Baker equivalent of "Gygaxian is not Gygax".
2. **Apocalypse World is a table game for three-to-five players and a human MC.** Several principles and
   moves assume *plural players who can look at each other*: "Go around the table", "Give every character
   good screen time with other characters", "Put it in the players' hands", "Turn questions back on the
   asker or over to the group at large" (`AW-REF` ●). This product is one player and a model. Ask whether a
   position solves a problem *this* game has before importing it; several do not, and the lens should recuse
   itself rather than stretch.
3. **Two principles depend on the MC *withholding on purpose*.** "Make your move, but misdirect" and "Make
   your move, but never speak its name" (`AW-REF` ●) describe an intention — the MC knows what they are
   doing and declines to name it. A language model has no such interior act. These transfer as *output
   shape* ("the fiction states the consequence, never the mechanism") and not as *stance*; note the
   downgrade whenever you apply them.
4. **The blog is thinking out loud, in public, with pushback.** anyway. posts are dated positions inside an
   argument, often revised in their own comment threads. Treat a blog line as "his position on that date",
   never as doctrine, and prefer the published game text where the two touch.
5. **His register is not a design position.** *Apocalypse World*'s profanity, sexual content and threat
   vocabulary are voice choices for that game's genre. Nothing in this lens imports them, and the lens has
   no opinion on this game's content settings.
6. **He designs for punishing outcomes on purpose, and says so.** "It may, in fact, be a personal failing to
   want unwelcome outcomes in your games" (`ANY-360` ●, his own footnote). Where this lens argues for cost,
   it is arguing from a stated taste that the owner is free to decline. Say "the lens wants X; the product
   chose Y" — not "the product is wrong".

## 1. The MC's agenda — what the GM is *for*

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Make the world seem real. | "Make Apocalypse World seem real." — `AW-REF` ● | The whole anti-drift stack (memory tiers, canon blocks, the clock, W2 identity) is this agenda item, mechanized. It is the game's strongest suit. Does anything *else* in the prompt outrank it? |
| 1.2 | Make the players' characters' lives not boring. | "Make the players' characters' lives not boring." — `AW-REF` ● | Not "make the story good", not "make the player happy" — make their *lives* not boring, which means trouble. Measure it: how many turns in a row can pass with nothing happening to the character? |
| 1.3 | Play to find out what happens. | "Play to find out what happens." — `AW-REF` ● | The three-act skeleton and the ending button pre-plan an arc (settled). The honest test is narrower: within a turn, does the GM *decide* the outcome before the fiction has settled it? |
| 1.4 | Those three are the *only* agenda items — not "get them through your storyline". | `DA-35` ◐ (AW text quoted on a fan blog) | Applied cautiously because of the source. Read it as a warning about the skeleton block's authority, not a verdict on the skeleton. |
| 1.5 | The MC is not the players' opponent, and says so out loud at the start. | "I'm not out to get you. I'm here to find out" — `AW-REF` ● | The product's answer to "can the GM be trusted" is the drift stack, not a declaration. Fine. But the inverse failure — a GM that has quietly stopped opposing anything — is the one this game actually risks. |

## 2. Always say

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Say what the principles demand, what the rules demand, what your prep demands, and what honesty demands. | `AW-REF` ● (the four-item "always say" list) | Four authorities, ranked by being listed. In this game: DEFAULT_RULES, the tag contract, the skeleton/blueprint, and the fiction as it actually stands. When two conflict in a turn, which wins, and is that written down anywhere? |
| 2.2 | "What honesty demands" is a *rule*, not a virtue. | `AW-REF` ● | The strongest single import for an LLM GM. A narrated risk that never lands, a foe that never swings, a rumour that never pays off — each is a dishonesty the rules were supposed to forbid. |
| 2.3 | The MC's job is to say *what happens*, then ask what the characters do next. | "says what happens, and asks you what your characters do next" — `PBTA-1` ● | This is the turn contract in one sentence. Check both halves against an actual turn: does the narration say what happened, and does it hand the initiative back? |

## 3. The principles — how the GM speaks

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Be a fan of the players' characters. | "Be a fan of the players' characters." — `AW-REF` ● | Fandom is not protection. A fan wants to see the character *tested*. Does the prose flatter the hero, or is it interested in what he'll do when it costs him? |
| 3.2 | Name everyone, make everyone human. | "Name everyone, make everyone human." — `AW-REF` ● | The NPC roster and naming clause do the first half well. The second half is the question: does a walk-on get a want, a fear, a reason to be standing there? |
| 3.3 | Address yourself to the characters, not the players. | "Address yourself to the characters, not the players." — `AW-REF` ● | The second-person contract and the person-drift note already enforce this at the sentence level. Where it slips is *meta* — a button label, a menu, an out-of-fiction handoff. |
| 3.4 | Ask provocative questions and build on the answers. | "Ask provocative questions and build on the answers." — `AW-REF` ● | The GM here almost never asks the player anything. What would it cost to let an NPC ask one real question a scene, and to have the answer become canon? |
| 3.5 | Look through crosshairs — see everything as a target. | "Look through crosshairs." — `AW-REF` ● | The clean structural test: for every standing thing in the fiction (an item, an ally, a reputation, a place), is there a state path by which it can be *taken*? |
| 3.6 | Respond with fuckery and intermittent rewards. | "Respond with fuckery and intermittent rewards." — `AW-REF` ● | *Intermittent* is the load-bearing word. Constant reward is the same signal as constant denial: no information. Measure the ratio, not the presence. |
| 3.7 | Think offscreen too — the world runs while the players aren't looking. | "Think offscreen too." — `AW-REF` ● | Schedules, futureEvents and NPC routines are the machinery for exactly this. Are they written during play, or only closed out? |
| 3.8 | Make your move, but misdirect; make your move, but never speak its name. | `AW-REF` ● | Downgraded per caution 0.3. As output shape: the fiction shows the consequence; it never narrates the machinery. The engine-notes protocol already enforces the strong form of this. |
| 3.9 | Sometimes, disclaim decision-making. | "Sometimes, disclaim decision-making." — `AW-REF` ● | See §6. This is the principle with the most direct mechanical analogue in this codebase, and the most under-used. |
| 3.10 | Leave yourself things to wonder about; look for where they're not in control, and push there. | `AW-REF` ● (during-session list) | "Push where they're not in control" is a *targeting* rule. Does anything in the prompt tell the GM where the character is weak — or does it only tell it what the character has? |
| 3.11 | Leap forward with named, human NPCs; hell, have a fight. | `AW-REF` ● | Both are permissions to *escalate on the MC's own initiative*. DEFAULT_RULES already grants both. The question is whether the surrounding machinery makes it easy or expensive. |

## 4. The MC's moves — the grammar of consequence

The full list, from the MC sheet (`AW-REF` ●). Fourteen fictional moves plus a handoff. Every one of them
happens *to the characters, in the fiction*. That is the property to test against this game's own move
registry.

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The MC has a closed list of moves, and every one is a fictional event. | "Separate them." · "Capture someone." · "Put someone in a spot." — `AW-REF` ● | This game has an engine-note registry (64 builders) that is structurally the same idea. How many of its entries move the *fiction* rather than the *records*? |
| 4.2 | Announce future badness. Announce off-screen badness. | `AW-REF` ● | Two moves whose entire content is *telling the player something bad is coming or has happened elsewhere*. Is there any engine surface that asks for this? |
| 4.3 | Tell them the possible consequences and ask. | "Tell them the possible consequences and ask." — `AW-REF` ● | The move that hands a decision back with its price attached. Compare the suggested-action buttons: do any of them carry a stated cost? |
| 4.4 | Offer an opportunity, with or without a cost. | "Offer an opportunity, with or without a cost." — `AW-REF` ● | "With or without" — the cost is optional but *available*. A menu where no option ever costs anything has lost half the move. |
| 4.5 | Take away their stuff. Activate their stuff's downside. Make them buy. | `AW-REF` ● | Three moves that operate on the inventory. The engine has ITEM_LOST and GOLD and a wares economy — is the GM ever *pointed* at them as a consequence, or only as bookkeeping? |
| 4.6 | Turn their move back on them. | "Turn their move back on them." — `AW-REF` ● | The elegant consequence: the player's own approach becomes the problem. Nothing in the prompt suggests it; nothing prevents it either. |
| 4.7 | Inflict harm; trade harm for harm — "as established". | `AW-REF` ● | *As established* means the harm number was fixed before the moment, so the MC can't soften it in flight. Are this game's damage numbers established in advance, or narrated after the fact? |
| 4.8 | Make a threat move — the specific move belonging to the specific threat. | `AW-REF` ● | The generic list is the fallback; the *threat's own* move list is the real content. This game has no per-antagonist move set. Should it? |
| 4.9 | After every move: "what do you do?" | "After every move: 'what do you do?'" — `AW-REF` ● | The single most testable clause in the whole rubric. Sample the last sentence of N turns. |
| 4.10 | Choosing a move: generally set up a future harder move and leave room to act and react; when handed a perfect opening, hit as hard as you like. | "set you up for a future harder move" · "give the players' characters some opportunity to act and react" · "hands you the perfect opportunity on a golden plate" — `AW-89` ◐ | The escalation ladder in one rule. Note the source is second-hand (caution 0.1). The question: does this game have *any* notion of graded consequence, or only success/failure? |

## 5. Threats — where the world's initiative lives

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Every threat carries an **impulse** — a standing, one-line want that drives it without the MC deciding. | "Slaver (to own and sell people)" · "Cannibal (craves satiety and plenty)" — `AW-THR` ● | The cheapest, highest-leverage idea in Baker's whole toolkit, and this game's closest miss. Companions carry `Wants:`; ordinary NPCs carry a *mood*. A mood is a reaction; an impulse is an engine. |
| 5.2 | Threats are kinds, and the kind supplies the move list. | Warlords / Grotesques / Brutes / Afflictions / Landscapes / Terrain / Vehicles — `AW-THR` ● | A registry of kinds each supplying behaviour is exactly the project's own "abstraction over scattered conditionals" rule. If per-NPC agency is ever built, build it as a table, not as prose. |
| 5.3 | The threat map is spatial and layered: PCs' own resources innermost, then "closer", then "farther", then "notional". | `AW-THR` ● | The location graph and the map viewer are the analogue. Does anything place *threats* on it, or only places? |
| 5.4 | Each threat gets a **stakes question** — the thing the MC honestly does not know the answer to. | "List its stakes question(s)." — `AW-THR` ● | The purest form of "play to find out". A campaign skeleton states what *will* happen; a stakes question states what is *undecided*. Does the skeleton block carry any undecided question at all? |
| 5.5 | Even landscapes and terrain get impulses and moves — the world itself acts. | "A precipice (to send someone over)" · "Shifting ground (to cost someone their bearing)" — `AW-THR` ● | Places in this game have descriptions, hours, wares and residents. None of them wants anything. |
| 5.6 | Essential threats are derived mechanically from the PCs' own attachments. | "For any PC's gang, create as brutes." — `AW-THR` ● | The threat list is generated *from what the character has to lose*. Compare: does anything in this engine read the character sheet looking for targets? |

## 6. Disclaiming decision-making — who decides

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | When something comes up you'd rather not decide by personal whim, don't — pass it off. | `AW-REF` ● (the decision-making box) | The one principle that maps *perfectly* onto an LLM GM, because "personal whim" is precisely the failure mode of a model narrating its own dice. |
| 6.2 | Tool one: put it in your NPCs' hands. | "Put it in your NPCs' hands." — `AW-REF` ● | Requires NPCs with standing wants (5.1). Without impulses, this tool is unavailable — the model has nothing to defer *to*. |
| 6.3 | Tool two: put it in the players' hands. | "Put it in the players' hands." — `AW-REF` ● | The buttons, the ending offer, the quest-acceptance rule and the item-canon proposal are four real instances. The game is genuinely good at this one. |
| 6.4 | Tool three: create a countdown. | "Create a countdown." — `AW-REF` ● | The campaign clock, SCHEDULE and futureEvents are countdowns already built. Are any of them *threatening* countdowns, or only calendar entries? |
| 6.5 | Tool four: make it a stakes question. | "Make it a stakes question." — `AW-REF` ● | See 5.4. Unbuilt here. |
| 6.6 | Some moves are the GM's to call, not the player's: "check" moves and "save" moves. | "The GM chooses and calls for the move, not the player." — `PBTA-5` ● | Useful vocabulary for this codebase: the model calling for a check *is* a check move; a foe's attack landing as a player save *is* a save move. Naming the three shapes would make the dice contract legible. |
| 6.7 | The GM's own resolution options are a graded list, not a coin flip. | "Tell them what goes wrong and what happens as a consequence." · "Tell them they aren't sure they'll be able to do it, and ask" — `PBTA-5` ● | Five options in Baker's example move, only one of which is "you do it". Compare a binary success/failed resolver. |

## 7. Fiction, system and the conversation

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | The core of the game is a structured conversation; everything else elaborates it. | "The innermost core is the structured conversation: you say what your characters do." — `PBTA-1` ● | A solo player typing into a box is a conversation with one very fast partner. The structure — who speaks, in what order, ending how — is the thing to audit. |
| 7.2 | Fictional causes have real-world effects; real-world causes have fictional effects. | "fictional causes have real-world effects. Real-world causes have fictional effects." — `PBTA-1` ● | The tag system is a literal implementation: fiction → tags → state → prompt → fiction. This is the game's deepest agreement with Baker, and it is well built. |
| 7.3 | The mechanical layer exists to give the fiction momentum, not to model it. | "give momentum to the fictional things" — `PBTA-1` ● | The test for any new tag or bible entry: does it *push* a scene, or only record one? A registry that only records is weight. |
| 7.4 | The layers should collapse gracefully inward — forget a rule and you land on the layer below. | `PBTA-1` ● | Directly applicable to a model that will drop instructions. When the GM forgets a tag, what does the engine fall back to — a repaired state, a note, or silent corruption? |
| 7.5 | The Lumpley Principle: system is the means by which the group agrees on imagined events. | Wording attributed to Baker and Emily Care Boss (◐; the standard citation pages could not be fetched — 403/500). Baker's own gloss: "how the real timeline reaches in and makes contact with the fictional one" — `ANY-695` ● | In this product the "group" is one player, one model and one parser, and the *parser* holds the final say. That is a real, defensible system. It should be stated as one, because it decides every dispute. |
| 7.6 | Fictional positioning is how the fiction reaches back and constrains the mechanics. | `ANY-695` ● | The affordance gate on suggestions and the "never invent doors, exits, items, or people" clause are positioning enforcement. Good. Does anything enforce it on the *narration*, or only the buttons? |
| 7.7 | Rules that refer to the fiction without pretending to simulate it ("effectivist") are the useful kind. | "The rules refer extensively to the fictional stuff but don't pretend to represent it" — `ANY-36` ● | A caution against bible sprawl: a rule earns its place by changing what happens next, not by describing the world more precisely. |
| 7.8 | A move can be triggered by a fictional event the player must *wait for*, not merely assert. | `PBTA-5` ● | Everything in this game triggers off the player's typed action or a turn counter. Nothing triggers off a fictional condition the world produces. That is a whole class of move the engine cannot express. |

## 8. Risk, dice and the point of having rules

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | The purpose of rules is to create the unwelcome and the unwanted. | "the purpose of an rpg's rules is to create the unwelcome and the unwanted" — `ANY-360` ● | The sharpest question in the file. What, in this engine, can produce an outcome that neither the player nor the GM would have chosen? |
| 8.2 | You play by rules because you want outcomes you would otherwise reject. | "outcomes that upset every single person at the table" — `ANY-360` ● | If every mechanism can be talked past in prose, the rules are decorative and honest freeform would be better. |
| 8.3 | If the rules only structure an agreement everyone already likes, live negotiation is better and the rules are waste. | `ANY-360` ● | A cost test for every new tag, note and bible field: what does it stop from happening? |
| 8.4 | The magic trick: combat that *feels* genuinely risky without killing PCs off all the time. | "make combat feel genuinely risky without killing PCs off all the time" — `ANY-759` ● | This is the plot-armour / death-walk design goal, stated by the person who named it. The product's settled ruling and Baker's stated goal agree. |
| 8.5 | The trick is not that bad outcomes are rare — it is that you choose *which* bad outcome you take. | "you get to make it the bad or terrible outcome you can live with" — `ANY-759` ● | The half this game has not built. Survival without a chosen sacrifice is not the magic trick; it is just survival. |
| 8.6 | A miss is not "nothing happens"; a partial is a real fork. | "On a miss, be prepared for the worst." · "a worse outcome, a hard bargain, or an ugly choice" — `AW-REF` ● | Three named partial-success shapes. A resolver with only `success` and `failed` cannot express any of them. |
| 8.7 | Even an information move that misses still gives the information, at a price. | "On a miss, ask 1 anyway, but be prepared for the worst." — `AW-REF` ● | The anti-stall rule. A failed check that returns nothing wastes a turn; a failed check that returns the answer plus a problem does not. |
| 8.8 | If nothing is at stake, say yes; roll only when something is. | `DOGS` ◐ (secondary; the book not read first-hand) | Applied as a question, never a verdict, per the source grade: when the engine or GM says *no*, was anything at stake in that refusal? |
| 8.9 | Escalation: a conflict can be raised to a costlier arena, and the cost of raising it is the point. | `DOGS` ◐ | The suggested-action buttons are the natural home for an escalation choice. Do any of the three ever offer to raise the stakes rather than continue at the same level? |

## 9. Where the lens would fight the product

Stated as dissent so a review can name it and move on. Settled owner rulings are marked; the lens does not
relitigate them.

- **The three-act skeleton is prep that knows the ending** (1.3, 5.4 ●). Baker's prep is threats with
  impulses and *questions*; this game's prep is acts with turning points. **Settled** — the skeleton and the
  ending button stay. The lens's residual, non-settled ask is smaller: give the skeleton one stakes question
  it genuinely does not answer.
- **Plot armour and the death walk** (8.4 ●). Here the lens mostly *agrees*: "without killing PCs off all
  the time" is Baker's own stated goal. **Settled**, and the settled ruling is the one Baker would sign.
  The dissent is only about 8.5 — survival should cost something the player picked.
- **The model narrates its own dice** (6.1 ●). "Disclaim decision-making" exists precisely to stop a GM
  deciding by whim, and a model rolling and interpreting its own die is the whim case in pure form.
  **Settled** — model-narrated dice stay. Record the dissent; the mitigations (a DC contract, an outcome
  vocabulary) are not settled and remain open.
- **A menu instead of a question** (4.9 ●). Three buttons are not "what do you do?"; they are "which of
  these three do you do?" **Settled** that the buttons stay. The non-settled question is whether the three
  are ever *different enough* to constitute a decision.
- **The MC as prose stylist** (`AW-REF` ●'s "Barf forth apocalyptica" is a *description* instruction, not a
  voice-imitation one). The prose-voice and author-DNA machinery is a craft this game invented for its own
  reasons; the lens has no documented position for or against it and should recuse itself, noting only that
  STYLE occupies the prompt's most load-bearing position and says nothing about consequence.
- **Solo play generally.** Four of the eleven principles and one of the four disclaim-tools assume other
  players in the room (caution 0.2). The lens should not score the game against them.

## 10. Things the lens must never say

- That Baker distinguishes "hard moves" from "soft moves". He does not; the terms are community coinage
  (caution 0.1).
- That "play to find out what happens" means the GM should have no prep. The same book demands a threat map,
  essential threats and impulses before the second session (`AW-THR` ●).
- That Baker opposes GM authority. The MC's moves are unilateral and constant; what he opposes is deciding
  by *whim* when a tool is available (6.1).
- That he wants PCs killed. "Make combat feel genuinely risky without killing PCs off all the time" is his
  own goal statement (`ANY-759` ●).
- That the Lumpley Principle means "rules don't matter". It means the opposite: whatever decides what
  happened *is* your system, so choose it deliberately (7.5).
- That "be a fan of the players' characters" means protecting them (3.1).
- That any of this is Baker's opinion of AI-run games. **No statement by Baker about an LLM acting as GM was
  found in this research. Do not invent one, and do not infer one from his remarks about tables.**
- Anything about Baker as a person, his views outside published design writing, or his collaborators'
  views. Meguey Baker co-wrote AW 2e and Under Hollow Hills; where a quoted line is from AW 2e it is
  credited to both, and this lens does not attribute it to Vincent alone.

## 11. Open verification gaps

- The full *Apocalypse World* text (1e or 2e) was not read. Everything from AW here comes from the two free
  PDFs on Baker's own site — the basic moves and MC sheets — which are summary sheets, not the chapter prose.
  The MC chapter's discussion of *when* to make a move is therefore unread.
- `AW-89` (the move-choosing guideline, including "golden plate") is quoted second-hand from a community
  archive. Verify against the book before any verdict leans on it.
- *Dogs in the Vineyard* was not read. "Roll dice or say yes", escalation and the raise/see cycle are all ◐
  from encyclopedic secondaries. Every Dogs position in §8 is therefore a question, never a verdict.
- *Under Hollow Hills* and *Murderous Ghosts* were not read; the brief named the former and this file makes
  no claim from it.
- The canonical Lumpley Principle wording could not be fetched from either standard citation page (403 and
  500 on 2026-09-07). The formulation is ◐; only Baker's own gloss in `ANY-695` is ●.
- daily-apocalypse.com is Jason D'Angelo's blog, not Baker's — an early assumption in this research that was
  caught and corrected. Anything sourced there is ◐ at best, and commentary there is D'Angelo's, not Baker's.
- PbtA essay parts 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 were not read; parts 1 and 5 only.

## 12. How a review uses this file

Name the surface, cite the entry (`4.9`, `8.5`), state the question, then the measured fact from the code,
prompt text or corpus, then the verdict — in the AUDIT_FABLE shape (finding, remedy, living status,
validation). Dissent from §9 is stated as dissent. Any finding that leans on a ◐ entry says so in the same
sentence. The review's first line carries the lens's own caveat: this is a rubric of published craft
positions, not the man.
