# The Jemisin lens — a sourced review rubric for Traffic and Dragons

**Read this when** a narration-person, worldbuilding, memory, oppression-content, structure or ending change wants
a second opinion from the author whose most famous book runs on the exact voice this product shipped. Research
deliverable of the TTRPG panel project (TODO #338; the Gygax lens is the shape this file matches).

**What this is.** A rubric of N. K. Jemisin's *documented* craft positions, each with a source, a confidence grade
and the review question it asks of this game. **What this is not:** an impersonation. A review that uses this file
speaks as "the Jemisin lens", never as Jemisin, and cites the entry it is applying. Any claim not in this file is
not a Jemisin position for our purposes. She is a living person: this rubric holds **public craft statements only**,
nothing personal.

## 0. Cautions — read before applying anything below

1. **She is on record against the design lineage this game is built on.** She names D&D specifically as the source
   of fantasy's appetite for systematized magic: "I blame D&D for systematizing so many things that don't need
   to be" (`MAGIC` ●), and asks "Is that all fantasy is? Thin storytelling papered over a players' guide?"
   (`MAGIC` ●). The capability bible, the mana economy and the canonical spell-rules block are exactly what that
   post objects to. The lens is therefore **a dissenting voice on mechanics by construction** (§4), and useful
   mainly on voice, world, memory and structure (§1–§3, §5–§10).
2. **She is a real tabletop player, and her play preference is not this game's default loop.** "a little tabletop
   back in college — Marvel Superheroes… and just a little D&D" (`GR` ●), and of that group: "My group back then
   was mostly into adventure with a lot of fighting" — which she says she wasn't much into (`GR` ●). As of 2023
   she was joining a new group and considering GMing for the first time (`GR` ●). Take her as an interested
   non-specialist on procedure and an expert on prose, world and structure.
3. **The webinar and the interviews disagree about where a world starts, and both are hers.** `WB` builds strictly
   physics-first (§2.1); in interview she says "I'm a character-focused writer" and the world follows (`EL` ●).
   The resolution she implies: the webinar is an exercise for generating a world cold, the interview describes
   what actually starts a novel. **Weight `WB` for tooling questions (a generator IS a cold-start exercise) and
   the interview for authoring questions.**
4. **Her second-person theory is the opposite of the folk theory.** Most people assume "you" makes the reader the
   character. She says it "interferes with identification" (`CW` ●) — see §1. A review that applies §1 without
   reading this caution will get the finding backwards.
5. **Two famous lines are unverified and must not be quoted.** The widely-circulated "manifestation of privilege"
   phrase did not appear in the reproduction actually fetched, and the "harvest syllables from food labels"
   naming method traces to no fetched source. Both are ○; see §12.
6. **No verified statement from her about AI-generated fiction exists in this file.** A search for one returned
   nothing attributable. **Do not invent her position on a machine narrator**, favourable or otherwise. Where the
   product's premise would obviously interest her, the lens asks a question and stops.

## 1. Second person and narrative distance — the question only this lens answers

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Second person is a **distancing** device. It does not make the reader the character. | "second person actually interferes with identification" (`CW` ●) | The prompt calls for "vivid second-person prose" and nothing more. Does the game know what the mode is *for*, or only that it is the mode? |
| 1.2 | The mechanism is a constant reminder that the character is not you — the video-game sprite. | "You're reminded at every turn that this character's… beliefs and feelings are not yours" (`CW` ●) | What are this game's sprites? The character sheet, the portrait, the named self in dialogue, the alignment readout. Are they visible often enough, and does the narration ever collapse them? |
| 1.3 | The design goal is a paradox held open: distant and intimate at once. | "I wanted something that felt simultaneously distant and intimate" (`CW` ●); "both distancing and intimate at the same time. You can't be this person" (`BIGIDEA` ●) | A turn that is only intimate reads as wish-fulfilment; only distant reads as a menu. Which failure does this game's prose actually drift toward over a hundred turns? |
| 1.4 | Its original purpose was to encode **dissociation** — a mind not all present. | "I also wanted them to feel the disassociation of her, the not-all-here of her" (`TRICK` ●); "a sense of detachment that would replicate Essun's level of disassociation" (`LH` ●) | This game's hero is present, competent and acting. The mode is being used for its intimacy alone. Is that a legitimate second use, or is it the mode doing half a job? |
| 1.5 | Second person is an **offer the reader may refuse**, not a claim on them. | "You have a choice. You can reject it or empathize" (`CW` ●) | When the GM writes a line of dialogue the player never typed, the offer becomes an assertion. How often, and does it matter? |
| 1.6 | It **spends trust the text has not yet earned**, and costs readers. | "your audience does not necessarily trust you at all" (`WE` ●); "second person generally doesn't sell too well" (`EL` ●) | Onboarding: the first ten turns are where the mode is most expensive. Is anything in the opening designed to repay that cost? |
| 1.7 | The positive criterion is **pull**, not insertion — the mode should feel like an oral storyteller holding a room. | "That is what a good use of second-person should feel like" (`SPIN` ●) | Car Mode is literally that: a voice holding a listener. The mode and that surface are natively matched. |
| 1.8 | She did not theorise the choice. It came out of test chapters and she fought it. | "I resisted it for a while" (`LS` ●); "The second person voice just happened to be the one that felt right" (`GOODREADS` ●); "I wrote a test chapter in the second person on a whim and it worked" (`EL` ●); "I always write test chapters" (`WE` ●) | A caution against over-justifying. The mode is settled here by owner ruling; the lens is not entitled to demand a rationale she did not herself supply. |
| 1.9 | High immersion — first *or second* person, single POV — sits at the top of her Immersion Pyramid, and its rule is **explain little**. | Slide: "First Person / Second Person / Single PoV" under High Immersion; "High Immersion: Explain little. The strangeness of the world is conveyed mostly through context" (`WB` ●) | This game is structurally at the top of that pyramid. Everything §7 asks follows from that one fact. |
| 1.10 | The pyramid's governing rule is counterintuitive: the stranger the world, the **tighter** the POV. | "My recommendation: increase immersion as difference increases" (`WB` ●) | Sword and sorcery is a near world. By her rule this game could have run third person and did not — which means it is spending immersion it does not strictly need. What is it buying? |

## 2. Worldbuilding — the build order

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Build strictly physical → biological → social → speculative → story. Nothing social is chosen before the physics that constrains it. | `WB` demo order: planet → continents → mountains → climate bands → biomes → people → characters and plot (`WB` ●) | `generateBlueprintDraft` asks for premise → acts → arcs → NPCs → locations. That is the reverse order. Does anything in the pipeline ever ask a physical question? |
| 2.2 | A world's people are **derived** from its conditions, not decorated onto them. | "Your story's people will have adapted to the above, too!" (`WB` ●); "Even one sociological difference can have profound effects" (`WB` ●) | Blueprint locations carry a `description` (2–4 sentences of canon). Is any of it causal, or is it all atmosphere? |
| 2.3 | Pick **three** sociological characteristics; the rest is derivation, not invention. | "Pick three sociological characteristics to start. The rest… will dictate the rest" (`WB` ●) | A generator that asked for three would produce a world it could keep deriving from for 2,000 turns. It currently asks for none. |
| 2.4 | Exactly **one** speculative element. This is a hard cap. | "No more! The world is weird enough already!" (`WB` ●) | Ancestries, spell tiers, deities, the deep-time ladder, a bestiary, the mana pool. The game is well past one. Which of these is load-bearing and which is inherited furniture? |
| 2.5 | The speculative element must **create problems**, never solve them. | "Don't make something that will solve all problems! New elements should create new problems" (`WB` ●) | Does any campaign's central strangeness cost the world something, or is magic purely a party capability? |
| 2.6 | Environment generates pressure; pressure generates culture; culture generates values, religion, kinship and medicine, in that order. | The demo's chain: build-for-tsunamis → light materials and stilts → "Veneration of resilence, appreciation of ephemerality" → religion → kinship → "Get better on your own. Survive if you can." (`WB` ●) | That last line is a *healing rule derived from geography*. This game's healing rule comes from D&D. Would a world-derived one be better fiction? |
| 2.7 | Geography must be reconstructable, not merely drawn. | "Think 'puzzle pieces'. If you have more than one continent, they should fit together" (`WB` ●) | The location graph is a travel record, not a geography. `map_viewer` renders a force layout because there is no real topology. Is that an honest limit or a gap? |
| 2.8 | The fear of the hidden 90% is what produces generic worlds. | "It implies that there's something scary or dangerous about that hidden 90%" (`WB` ●); "Easier to copy than create" (`WB` ●), naming "medieval northern Europe ad infinitum" as the failure set | The default tone list is High Fantasy / Gritty / Sword-and-Sorcery / Horror / Intrigue. Every one of them is a *register*, none is a world. Where is the iceberg? |
| 2.9 | Research is real work, and it has a stop condition. | "Beware research addiction! Set a time limit for the research phase" (`WB` ●); "Use enough science to make sense to the layperson, and no more" (`WB` ●) | The prompt is a research library the GM reads every turn. What is *its* stop condition? |
| 2.10 | Do not stereotype; social science is science. | "DO NOT rely on stereotypes or myths" (`WB` ●); "Don't forget the social sciences!" (`WB` ●); "Social sciences are sciences too" (`ATL` ◐) | Every author `contentDNA` string is a *narrative* directive. None is a sociological one. |
| 2.11 | The world is not the story, and finishing it is not finishing. | "That was just the world. You still need to select characters and build a plot!" (`WB` ●) | The deck ends by deriving *two different books* — one low-immersion six-POV epic, one high-immersion single-POV saga — from the same world. This game is the second shape. |

## 3. Power, oppression and who does the work

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Power dynamics is a **worldbuilding step**, not a theme note. | `MC` lesson list carries "Micro-worldbuilding: Power Dynamics and Cultural Appropriation" as a named chapter between culture and magic (`MC` ◐, titles only) | Nothing in the blueprint schema, the skeleton schema or `DEFAULT_RULES` asks who holds power in this world or how they keep it. |
| 3.2 | Oppression must be **structural and self-maintaining**, not an evil overlord. | "It is not always a case of an evil overlord coming in and saying…" (`LS` ●); "you've got people complicit in the system who are part of it themselves" (`LS` ●) | The three-act skeleton's spine is a villain. Can this engine represent a harm with no antagonist to kill? |
| 3.3 | It is not allegory. Refusing a one-to-one mapping is the ethical choice. | "I was not exploring a specific oppression" (`WE` ●); "So that's why it doesn't map. I didn't want it to map" (`WE` ●) | A campaign generator that reaches for "slavers" as a stock antagonist is doing the opposite: borrowing the weight without the structure. |
| 3.4 | Oppression is not a default setting of societies. | "There have been cultures in our world that do not consider anyone expendable" (`CORNELL` ●) | Does the generator ever produce a world whose central problem is not somebody's cruelty? |
| 3.5 | A society is legible as a **resource-allocation system**, labour included. | On a comm: "modular, with every resource — food, weapons, labor, knowledge — meant to be shuffled around" (`GR` ●) | This is her describing her own world *for a roleplaying game*. It is the single most directly transferable sentence in the file. |
| 3.6 | Restoration-shaped fantasy is the tell. | "its job is to restore what is perceived to be out of whack" (`GUARD` ◐); "As a black woman I have no particular interest in maintaining the status quo" (`GUARD` ◐) | See §9. This is the ending question in worldbuilding form. |
| 3.7 | The default world is a choice, made invisible by repetition. | "When was the last time you considered the impact of a white writer's race" (`TOLKIEN` ●); worlds that "just seem strangely denuded" (`GUARD` ◐) | `DEFAULT_RULES` already forces ancestry variety by rule. That is the same instinct, mechanised. |
| 3.8 | "Race" is social, not biological, and traits are derived from conditions. | "race in our own world is a social construct, not anything related to actual biology" (`RACES` ●); creatures made as "mythological creatures sans mythology" to strip reader baggage (`RACES` ●) | `ANCS` gives ancestries stat modifiers. That is biology-as-race, the inherited D&D shape. Owner-settled as game furniture; the lens notes it and moves on. |

## 4. Magic and rules — the dissent

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Systematized magic is a category error, and gaming is why fantasy wants it. | "game logic should not apply to magic, because it's fucking magic" (`MAGIC` ●); "I blame D&D for systematizing so many things that don't need to be" (`MAGIC` ●) | Her direct objection to `capability_bible.js`. State it as dissent; do not act on it (§11). |
| 4.2 | The model she admires is unrepeatable, unpredictable magic. | Earthsea's magic: "never repeatable, never predictable" (`MAGIC` ●) | Is there anything in this game that magic cannot be relied on to do? |
| 4.3 | **But** the people inside a world absolutely do systematize their own power — that is realism, not authorial rulemaking. | "It's the laws of physics there. It's science for them" (`EL` ●); "They quantify to whatever degree they can. They treat it mechanistically" (`EL` ●) | This is the reconciliation. The bible is legitimate as *the culture's* mechanistic understanding — provided the prose never narrates it as a rulebook. `DEFAULT_RULES`' "CANON IS NOT CONVERSATION" is that fence. Is it holding? |

## 5. Structure and the braid

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | The shape was known from day one. Structure is planned, not discovered. | "I knew at the beginning that all three perspectives were the same person" (`WE` ●) | The three-act skeleton generated before play is the same instinct. Nothing here objects to it. |
| 5.2 | Structure is an **empathy-financing instrument** — it lends the protagonist goodwill before the reader knows they are lending it. | "So I decided to trick readers into caring about her" (`TRICK` ●); "'cash in' on the empathic capital built by the younger versions of Essun" (`TRICK` ●) | What does this game's structure *finance*? The skeleton finances plot coherence. Nothing finances attachment to a companion or a place. |
| 5.3 | Write the anchor first, then interleave. | She drafted the bulk of Essun before alternating, "Because I needed to have her lodged in my head" (`WE` ◐, paraphrase of a long passage) | Companion sheets and party histories are generated at recruit time, not before. The anchor is the player; everyone else is improvised in. |
| 5.4 | Parallelism must be dialled **down**. Echoes that announce themselves are a fault. | "I had to actually kind of stop that, because it was a little too obvious" (`WE` ●) | The engine's audits and nudges all push the GM to *state* the connection. Does anything push the other way? |
| 5.5 | Withholding is a reward for close reading, seeded for the reread, not a trick. | "a blanket which she mentions in one chapter that you're not going to see again" (`WE` ●) | `futureEvents` are promises the engine will chase. Is there any seed the engine deliberately does *not* chase? |

## 6. Memory and the unreliable past

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Knowledge is not stable. It decays and mutates under the pressure of survival. | beliefs and knowledge "shift over time and under the pressure of survival" (`LH` ◐ — the interviewer's rendering of her design) | The memory stack has three registers — record, memory, rumour. Are they actually kept apart, or does one leak into another? |
| 6.2 | She keeps *rewriting* the past across a trilogy: each volume adds a perspective that reveals how the world became what it is. | "we will see yet another perspective that will see into the past" (`CW` ◐) | `memory.eras` compresses played chapters into deep background. Does anything ever *re-read* an era differently in light of what came later? |
| 6.3 | Nostalgia is a lens that distorts, and she names it as such. | "rather than through the foggy lenses of nostalgia and privilege" (`HUGO16` ●) | See §9. The past being *wanted back* is the failure, not the past being lost. |

**Note.** Her forgotten-past architecture (stonelore, dead civilisations, unrecorded Seasons) is real and central to the
books, but **no interview was located in which she explains it in her own words**. §12 records this as a gap. Do not
attribute a theory of memory to her beyond 6.1–6.3.

## 7. Exposition, names and glossaries

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | At high immersion you explain little; strangeness arrives through context. | "High Immersion: Explain little… conveyed mostly through context, and rarely in the narrative" (`WB` ●) | The single most applicable line in the file, given §1.9. |
| 7.2 | Frequent explanation, direct address and prologues belong at **low** immersion, and carry a named hazard. | Slide: "Low Immersion: Explain frequently… Prologues are common. Danger: infodumping!" (`WB` ●) | Every block in the system prompt is an explanation. They are addressed to the GM, not the player — which is the right architecture. Where does one leak into prose? |
| 7.3 | Glossaries and maps are **spoilers**; hers go at the back. | She asks publishers to put them at the back because, like maps, they leak where the story goes; "I prefer for readers to figure that out the way the characters do" (`GLOSS` ●) | The player-facing sheet, quest journal and map viewer are exactly front-matter. Is any of it telling the player something the fiction has not? |
| 7.4 | Underwriting is coy; **overwriting is condescending**. | "Because past a certain point, I find underwriting coy. Overwriting is condescending" (`WE` ●); "It is assuming that your audience lacks the intelligence to figure out simple stuff" (`WE` ●) | The `STYLE` block already says a short reply is the right reply when little happened. This is that rule's author-side justification. |
| 7.5 | Names: no verified method. | ○ — the widely repeated "harvest syllables from labels and filenames" account traces to no source that was fetched | The `AVAILABLE NAMES` pool and the naming clause are engineering answers to an identity problem, not craft answers. The lens has nothing sourced to say; it asks only whether the pool ever produces a name that means something in-world. |

## 8. Violence, atrocity and the marginalised

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | The worst violence in the book is the part she did not invent — it is anchored to historical record. | Syenite killing her child is built on the Margaret Garner case, the history behind *Beloved* (`WE` ●, her account runs long) | Her answer to gratuitousness is *provenance*. Does this game's cruelty come from anywhere, or is it generated to fill a stakes slot? |
| 8.2 | The cost must be shown and the choice must be motivated. That is all the author owes. | "all I can do is show that there are reasons for those choices" (`WE`/`EL` ●) | A harm narrated and then never referenced again fails this. The core-memory tier is the engine's only mechanism for a cost that persists. |
| 8.3 | She is candid that authorial cruelty is partly appetite, and does not launder it as necessity. | "in my deepest soul, I am a sadist" (`WE` ●) | An honest position that forbids the opposite dishonesty: never justify a brutal beat as "the world demanded it". |
| 8.4 | Her stated craft anxiety is **didacticism**, not squeamishness; revision is where the brick becomes a pebble. | "a person that throws bricks at the heads of my readers" (`WE` ●); "Let's turn that brick to the head into a pebble to the head" (`WE` ●) | An LLM GM has no revision pass. Whatever it throws, it throws at full weight. What plays the role of revision here? |
| 8.5 | She disclaims message-writing outright. | "I'm not trying to create messages with the fiction I write" (`CW` ●) | A guard against reading this whole section as a demand for moral content. It is a demand for *structure* and *cost*, not for a lesson. |

## 9. Agency, restoration and endings

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9.1 | The named failure is not the passive protagonist. It is the **nostalgic restoration plot**. | "You want to get back to the world as it was" — and if it was a shithole, "you have no need to go back" (`WE` ●) | Does this engine's ending machinery return the world to its opening state, or leave it changed? |
| 9.2 | Endings should open forward. ## 11. Where the lens would fight the product

These argue against parts of Traffic and Dragons that are validated or owner-settled. They are here so a review can
state the dissent honestly and then say why the product disagrees — not so the dissent wins by default.

- **Mechanized magic** (§4.1). Her objection is direct, published, and names D&D as the culprit. The product's
  answer is that the bible is an **anti-drift** device, not a player-facing rulebook: it exists because an LLM
  narrator will otherwise invent a spell's range differently every turn. Her own §4.3 supplies the reconciliation —
  the *culture* systematizes. The live test is whether the prose ever narrates a rule as a rule.
- **One speculative element** (§2.4). The game is far past one and will not go back. This is a cost the product
  pays knowingly for genre legibility; the lens should ask which elements *earn* their prompt weight, not demand
  deletion.
- **Explain little** (§7.1) versus an engine that explains constantly. The product's answer is that the explaining
  is addressed to the GM and fenced from the prose by rule. The lens should audit the fence, not the library.
- **Stat-modifying ancestries** (§3.8). Biology-as-race is the inherited shape and she is explicitly against the
  premise. Owner-settled game furniture; record and move on.
- **The clerical-image ban** (settled). Her worldbuilding runs on who owes whom and who eats (§3.5). The ban is on
  *paperwork as plot and metaphor*, not on economy as substance — and the product replaced the banned register with
  better instruments (whispers, wares, wanted, residents, hours). The lens should watch the substitution, never
  reopen the ban.
- **Second-person present, always** (settled). She chose the mode once, for one book, for a reason specific to that
  character (§1.4), and names its costs (§1.6). She would not say a *product* should be permanently in it. The
  product's answer is that it is a single-player, single-POV, spoken-aloud medium — the top of her own pyramid
  (§1.9) — and that Car Mode is the oral-storyteller case she praises (§1.7). Record the dissent as settled.

## 12. Things the lens must never say

- That "you" makes the player become the character. She says the opposite (§1.1).
- That she chose second person for a theorised effect. She resisted it and called it instinct (§1.8).
- That she demands rule-free magic in *every* context. Her own worlds' cultures quantify obsessively (§4.3).
- That she opposes planned structure or outlines. She knew the reveal on day one (§5.1).
- That she wants fiction to carry a message. She disclaims it (§8.5).
- That she opposes maps or glossaries. She makes them; she wants them at the back (§7.3) and made maps to track
  fault lines (`EL` ●).
- That worldbuilding starts with a map. It starts with a planet's physics, and a novel starts with a character
  (§2.1, caution 3).
- The "manifestation of privilege" quote, or the syllable-harvesting naming method. Neither is verified (§13).
- Anything at all about her views on AI narrators, LLM prose, or this product's premise. No source exists.
- Anything personal. Public craft statements only.
