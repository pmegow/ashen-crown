# The Laws lens — a sourced review rubric for Traffic and Dragons

**Read this when** a pacing, information-flow, suggestion-button, scene-structure or failure-handling change
wants a second opinion from the designer who spent twenty years asking what a *scene* is for. Research
deliverable of the TTRPG panel project (owner ruling 2026-09-05: research only; the panel skill is TODO #338).

**What this is.** A rubric of Robin D. Laws' *documented* design positions — GUMSHOE, DramaSystem/Hillfolk,
*Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering*, *Hamlet's Hit Points* / *Beating the Story*, the See Page XX columns,
his blog and LiveJournal, and interviews — each with a source, a confidence grade and the review question it
asks of this game. **What this is not:** an impersonation. A review that uses this file speaks as "the Laws
lens", never as Laws, and cites the entry it is applying. Anything not in this file is not a Laws position
for our purposes. He is a living person; only public statements about craft and design are in scope.

**Confidence legend.** ● primary (his own published words, read by me at the cited URL) · ◐ secondary
(attributed by a reliable source, or his words reported inside a third party's account) · ○ uncertain or
unverified. A ○ position may inform a question, never a verdict. Quotes are capped at fifteen words.

## 0. Cautions — read before applying anything below

1. **His subject is the table, not the medium.** Every position below was written for a human GM with human
   players in a room. Where a position depends on *reading the room* (SLY, STEW), this game has no room to
   read — the engine has state, not faces. The lens must translate, or recuse itself, rather than scoring the
   product against a channel it does not have.
2. **He is a working designer whose positions are tool-shaped.** GUMSHOE's core-clue rule solves a specific
   failure of *investigative* scenarios; it is not a universal claim that dice should never gate anything.
   Ask what problem a position was built for before importing it (SRD, XX-gimme).
3. **The beat vocabulary is an analysis tool for finished stories, applied backwards to prep.** *Hamlet's Hit
   Points* maps films and plays; the RPG application is the GM stocking "a bunch of things that might leap in
   as an upward or downward beat" (SLY ◐). Reading a play transcript as a screenplay is his own method, but a
   verdict that a session "should have had a bringdown at turn 30" over-applies it.
4. **Robin's Laws (2002) is read here through reviewers.** I could not open a copy. The player-type taxonomy
   and the improvisation procedure below are graded ◐ and traced to close readings (CH2, CH8), not to the
   book's pages. Do not quote the book directly from this file.
5. **He is not a lethality or economy theorist.** He has little published on XP curves, encumbrance, treasure
   or death spirals. The lens has nothing useful to say about the XP curve, inventory size, or the class
   bible, and should say so rather than improvise.
6. **He is on record as pro-collaboration, not pro-permissiveness.** "The History of No" (XX-no) argues
   against reflexive refusal; it is not an argument that the GM should grant every request. A lens finding
   that the engine "says no too much" needs the specific refusal, not the vibe.

## 1. Information flow and clues

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Gating necessary information on a roll is the named failure: the story stops. | "If you roll well, you get the clue. If not, you don't—and the story grinds to a halt." — SRD ● | Is there any path in this game where a check failure withholds a fact the story needs? The `[CHECK:]` tag, `[DICE:]` narration, the skills ladder. |
| 1.2 | Each scene has a core clue the players cannot miss; only the interpretation is at risk. | "For each scene, the GM designates a core clue… you will never fail to gain a piece of necessary information" — SRD ● | The GM here has no scene-level concept of "the one thing they must leave with". Does anything in the prompt distinguish load-bearing information from colour? |
| 1.3 | The point of an investigation is interpreting what you found, not finding it. | "Investigative scenarios are not about finding clues, they're about interpreting the clues you do find." — SRD ● | Do the suggestion buttons ever offer an *interpretive* move (conclude, accuse, decide what it means), or only acquisition moves (ask, search, examine)? |
| 1.4 | Reserve hard difficulties for optional benefits, never for plot-critical steps. | "Reserve especially hard Difficulty Numbers for obstacles which provide interesting but nonessential benefits." — SRD ● | The MECHANICS line teaches DC 10/15/20 with no doctrine about what may sit behind a 20. Is a plot gate ever priced at DC 20? |
| 1.5 | Clues arrive two ways: on entering the scene, or when a player *does* something. | "All an investigator with the governing ability has to do to spot the clue is to enter the scene." — XX-clue ● | The whispers note is the engine's "enter the scene" channel. Is there a second, action-dependent channel, or does every fact require the player to ask a question? |
| 1.6 | Clue dispensation is adaptive: watch progress and change the delivery mid-scenario. | "the most important thing about clue dispensation is to pay attention to the progress players are making" — XX-clue ● | The engine observes many drift axes. Does anything observe whether the player is *stuck* — and change what the GM offers when they are? |
| 1.7 | A credible alternate method always works; the fixed clue is a floor, not a script. | "players can always get the clue if they present you with a credible alternate method" — XX-two ● | The affordance gate rejects impossible buttons. Does it ever reject a *player-typed* creative approach, and if so, what does the GM see? |
| 1.8 | Exposition should be smuggled inside a scene that is doing something else. | "Ideally, exposition is conveyed in a seamless way, slipped into a scene that does something else." — SCENE ● | Measure the LORE tags: how many arrived during an action, and how many arrived because the player asked a question? |
| 1.9 | Three information beat types carry the tease: pipe, question, reveal. | pipe "surreptitiously plants information which at the time seems tangential"; question "introduces a mystery we want to see solved"; reveal "orienting us and reducing our sense of confusion" — INFO ● | The engine has FUTURE_EVENT (a promise), LORE (a reveal) and DECISION. It has no *pipe*: a fact planted now whose relevance lands later. Is the futureEvents queue that, or is it only a question? |

## 2. Beats, pacing and the shape of a session

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The two load-bearing beat types are dramatic (personal interaction) and procedural (external obstacles). | "dramatic beats of personal interaction and procedural beats in which characters overcome external obstacles" — BEAT ● | Classify a real run's turns. What is the actual ratio, and does anything in the engine measure it? |
| 2.2 | A satisfying narrative is a modulated but gradually downward line, redeemed at the end. | "it fits the pattern of most satisfying narratives—a modulated but gradually downward line" — BEAT ● | The campaign runs 2,437 turns with an ending button at the end of act three. Is there any downward pressure across an act, or does each turn reset to neutral? |
| 2.3 | Oscillate between hope and fear; vary the rhythm rather than sustaining one register. | "Oscillate between hope and fear" — SLY ◐ | The DEFAULT_RULES demand danger in every scene. A constant-fear register is as flat as a constant-hope one. What supplies the *up*? |
| 2.4 | Flourish beats — commentary, bringdown, gratification, anticipation — work only when rationed. | "oddball flourish beats, like strong spices, register most satisfyingly when used sparingly" — BEAT ● | The montage and the "previously on" recap are flourish instruments. What rations them, and is the ration a story signal or a turn counter? |
| 2.5 | Anticipation is manufactured by telling the audience the hero *can* win this one. | "you have to let the players know as a given that they can overcome the opposition" — Laws, quoted in SHORT ◐ | Does the GM ever signal a fight is winnable before it starts? The bestiary and threat fields exist; is the *player* ever told? |
| 2.6 | Prep is a stock of beats, not a plan: things ready to leap in upward or downward. | "a bunch of things that might leap in as a upward or downward beat" — SLY ◐ | The futureEvents queue and the schedule are exactly this stock. Are they drawn on when the curve is flat, or only when their time arrives? |
| 2.7 | Combat, interaction and exploration map onto procedural, dramatic and informational beats. | SLY ◐ (Laws mapping D&D's three pillars) | The game has combat and exploration instrumented (COMBAT_*, LOCATION). Interaction has no beat instrument at all — only relationship tags recorded after the fact. |

## 3. Procedural versus dramatic scenes

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | The hobby is good at the procedural and bad at the dramatic; that gap is what DramaSystem exists for. | "In roleplaying we've always done the first really well… The second, not so much." — BAKER ● | Count the offered actions. If the buttons are a procedural menu, the game has inherited the gap he named. |
| 3.2 | A dramatic scene is one character seeking an emotional reward from another. | Hillfolk: dramatic scenes "revolve around emotional rewards and responses" ◐ (search-surfaced from the Hillfolk text; not read first-hand) | Companion "wants" (#330/#347) are the seed of this. Is there any *scene shape* for pursuing one, or only a note that it exists? |
| 3.3 | Characters are built as contradictions — two poles they can plausibly swing between. | "the two contradictory emotional impulses she's torn between" — POLES ● | The sheet has trait, flaw, motivation and an alignment drift pair. Are any of them *opposed* to each other, or are they four independent adjectives? |
| 3.4 | The emotional economy should push a character to yield about half the time. | "encourages you, in an emotional confrontation, to give in about half the time" — BAKER ● | Companion refusal is capped at "once per arc, never leaves". That is a yield rate near 100%. Is that the intended feel, or an artifact of safety? |
| 3.5 | Obstacles are better when they activate a PC's internal conflict or reshape a relationship. | "Construct obstacles so that they activate a PC's internal conflicts, or provide the opportunity to alter relationships" — Laws quoted in SHORT ◐ | When the GM invents an obstacle, does it see the character's flaw and the party's relationship axes as *material*, or only as description? |

## 4. Dice, failure and the gimme

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Before rolling, ask whether the roll generates suspense and whether anyone cares. | "Will this die roll generate suspense? Do the players really care all that much?" — XX-gimme ● | The GM narrates its own rolls by default. What decides *when* it rolls at all? Nothing in the prompt asks the suspense question. |
| 4.2 | Failure is only interesting when it is *interesting*; the threat of failure carries the scene, not the fact. | "Failure is usually boring. It is the credible but unrealized threat of failure that is interesting." — XX-gimme ● | Find the run's failures. Did each cost something a player would notice, or did it cost only a turn? |
| 4.3 | A gimme keeps the characters cool and competent and preserves momentum. | "Preserve momentum. Keep the characters cool and competent. It's a gimme." — XX-gimme ● | An engine-authored button that the fiction then refuses is the anti-gimme: it makes the hero look foolish for free. |
| 4.4 | A player may still roll on a gimme — for a garnish, never for the outcome. | "the player may still roll — to garnish his guaranteed success with a minor, additional benefit" — XX-gimme ● | The optional player-rolls-dice setting is exactly this shape when the check is not load-bearing. Is it ever used that way, or only for pass/fail? |
| 4.5 | The system removes boring failure to make room for interesting failure — it does not remove failure. | "It takes out the boring, stupid failure in order to have more interesting failures." — GN ● | The strongest single line for this product. Which failures does the engine *remove* (the death walk, plot armor), and did anything more interesting take their place? |
| 4.6 | Ask whether failure would move the plot as much as success; if yes, roll. | "Will failure be at least as interesting… as success?" — XX-gimme ● | A test the GM prompt could state in one clause and currently does not. |

## 5. Improvisation, prep and saying yes

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Be ready to throw out your plan for what the players are actually making. | "always be ready to jettison what you thought would happen in favor of what the players are making happen" — BAKER ● | The skeleton block says the opposite: "Do not invent unrelated side-plots that pull away from the current arc." Both are defensible; the tension should be deliberate. |
| 5.2 | Improvisation is a procedure, not a nerve: imagine the obvious, the challenging, the surprising and the pleasing outcome, then pick. | CH8 ◐ (close reading of *Robin's Laws*) | A four-way outcome sweep is a promptable procedure. The engine currently gives the GM the *facts* and no *method* for choosing what happens next. |
| 5.3 | Ask, in the moment: what is an interesting thing to happen next in this story? | "what is an interesting thing to happen next in the story?" — SLY ◐ | The nearest thing in the prompt is "DRIVE THE ADVENTURE". Is that a method or an exhortation? |
| 5.4 | Reflexive refusal is a habit worth unlearning; find a way to say yes with a complication. | XX-no ● (the "yes, but" columns are the same argument) | The affordance gate is a machine for saying no. It is fail-closed by design and correct — but is the *player* ever told why, or does the button simply vanish? |
| 5.5 | Confidence is the GM's biggest single lever. | "Confidence is a huge contributing factor to GM success." — STEW ● | Not applicable to a model. Recuse. |

## 6. Player types and what each wants

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Seven types — power gamer, butt-kicker, tactician, specialist, method actor, storyteller, casual gamer — each after a different emotional kick. | CH2 ◐ (*Robin's Laws*, read through a close reading) | This is a one-player product with a persistent sheet. Which type is it built for? The evidence says storyteller and method actor; the buttons say tactician. |
| 6.2 | Build each session so every player sees one of their traits addressed at least once. | CH2 ◐ | With one player the rule collapses to: does every session address more than one *mode*? A run of twenty ask-and-search turns addresses one. |
| 6.3 | Read the character sheet as the player's order for the kind of fun they want. | "character sheets as orders for the type of fun they'd like to have" — STEW ● | The sheet carries trait, flaw, motivation, alignment, skills and a want-bearing party. | 6.4 | Players should have a reason to be adventuring with the group. | SLY ◐ | Companions have wants; the hero's own motivation is on the sheet. Is the hero's motivation ever pressed by the fiction? |

## 7. Choice architecture and scenario breadth

| # | Position | Evidence | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | A scenario offering real freedom must contain more material than any one run can touch. | "A scenario that provides freedom and choices must include more material than any single group could possibly activate" — XX-two ● | The three-act skeleton is written to be *completed*. Does it carry unreachable material, or is every act goal on the critical path? |
| 7.2 | Unused prep is the design working, not waste. | XX-two ● | Does anything in the engine treat an unvisited arc or an unresolved futureEvent as a failure to be swept? |
| 7.3 | Branching means the GM improvises whole scenes, not just details. | "the GM may have to improvise new material, from minor details to whole scenes and branches" — XX-two ● | The GM is a model that improvises by default. | 7.4 | A published scenario is a blueprint for someone else's story, not the story. | "You're just laying out a blueprint for someone else to take and grab and make their own story." — GN ● | The blueprint format is literally this. | 7.5 | Witnesses answer questions; they do not deliver exposition in bulk. | XX-two ● (the "Lou answers specific questions as follows" scenario-text pattern) | The game does this well and possibly too well: nearly every information beat is a question answered. |

## 9. Where the lens would fight the product

Stated so a review can record the dissent honestly rather than letting it win by default.

- **A single player has no table to read.** Half his GM craft — spotlight balance, reading reactions,
  catering to seven types at once (6.1, 6.2, SLY) — presupposes a group. The product's answer is that the
  sheet and the save are the readable surface. The lens should press on whether the engine actually *reads*
  them when choosing what happens, not on whether it has faces to read.
- **"Every scene, quest and encounter should serve this story"** (buildSkeletonBlock) is the opposite of 7.1
  and 5.1. His scenario design wants surplus material and a GM ready to abandon the plan. The product's
  answer is that a 2,400-turn solo campaign with no human GM needs a spine or it dissolves. Legitimate — but
  it is a departure from him, not an application of him.
- **The dramatic scene has no mechanism here.** DramaSystem exists because he judged that RPGs cannot get
  emotional confrontation to happen reliably without one (3.1, 3.4). This game has relationship *data* and no
  dramatic *procedure*. The lens will keep returning to this; the product may reasonably answer that the GM
  model improvises dramatic scenes without a subsystem — but then the evidence should show them happening.
- **Death that cannot happen removes the fear half of hope/fear** (2.3 against the settled death walk). The
  product's counter is that a solo campaign with 2,400 turns of accumulated state cannot afford a permanent
  loss, and that capture, cost and consequence carry the fear instead. The lens should then ask for the
  measured cost of a down beat, not for lethality.
- **He would not treat prose voice as the product.** His writing is almost entirely about *structure* —
  beats, scenes, information. A finding of his shape is never "the prose is good"; it is "the shape of the
  hour was flat".

## 10. Things the lens must never say

- That GUMSHOE proves dice should not gate anything. It removes rolls from *finding required information*
  (SRD, 1.1); combat, general abilities and interpretation still roll.
- That he opposes failure. He removes "boring, stupid failure" for *more interesting failures* (GN, 4.5).
- That the core-clue rule is railroading. He argues it is the opposite — it is what makes a branching
  investigation runnable (XX-two, 7.1).
- That the beat vocabulary is a scoring rubric. It is an analysis language; *Hamlet's Hit Points* maps
  finished stories and the RPG use is prep stocking (SLY, caution 3).
- That the seven player types are his taxonomy of people. They are a GM's planning tool and he treats them as
  overlapping (CH2).
- That "yes, and" is his rule. He writes "yes, but" — the complication is the point (XX-no, the improv columns).
- Anything about him personally. Craft and design statements only.

## 11. Open verification gaps

- ***Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering* (2002) was not read.** Player types (§6), the eight-step
  improvisation procedure (5.2) and the "fun is the point / designers supply ~30%" framing all come through
  reviewers (CH2, CH8, rpg.net review snippets). The rpg.net review pages 302-redirect to the forum root and
  could not be opened at all. Every §6 entry is ◐ for that reason; the ~30/70 split is unsourced enough that
  it is **not** an entry in this file.
- ***Hamlet's Hit Points* and *Beating the Story* were not read.** The beat taxonomy comes from Laws' own
  blog and LiveJournal posts about the method (BEAT, INFO, SCENE ●) plus Emily Short's reading (SHORT ◐). The
  book's own definitions of the nine beats, and any claim about their proportions, are unverified.
- **The GUMSHOE SRD was read via the Open Gaming Network mirror**, not Pelgrane's PDF (which returned raw
  binary to the fetcher). The text is OGL-licensed and consistent across sources, but the quotes in §1 have
  not been checked against the Pelgrane original.
- **Hillfolk's own text was not read.** 3.2 (dramatic scenes as emotional-reward seeking) and the
  petitioner/granter mechanics rest on search-surfaced summaries plus the BAKER interview. Graded ◐.
- **Feng Shui positions are absent.** The cinematic-stunt design ("if your character needs a prop for a
  stunt, the prop is there") appeared only in third-party summaries; unverified, so no entry.
- **Humour and tone: no position.** Nothing published by Laws on comedy in RPGs was located, despite *Og* and
  his reputation for dry humour. The brief asked for humour; this file honestly has nothing. Do not invent one.
- **No Laws statement about AI game masters was found**, and none should be inferred. His remarks on
  collaboration and reading the room are about human tables.
- **"My Top Game Mastering Tip"** (Pelgrane, 2020) is a video; the page carries no transcript, so the tip
  itself is unrecorded here.

## 12. How a review would use this file

Name the member, the surface, and the entries applied. For each entry: the question, the finding against
actual code, prompt text or corpus turn, and the verdict, in the AUDIT_FABLE shape (finding, remedy, living
status, validation). Dissent from §9 is stated as dissent. The review's first line carries the caveat: this
is a rubric of documented positions, not the man.
