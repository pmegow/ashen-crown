Where the lenses converge — six themes, each reached by more than one seat

Stakes are stated fluently and settled at zero

Baker 1 · Harper 2, 4 · Abercrombie 2 · Leiber 4, 6 · Laws 4
Four lenses measured the same thing from four rubrics: across ninety turns four HP losses, one gold outflow, two conditions, zero alignment moves, and a Level-17 hero at full health through five late-game combat turns. The DRIVE rule already says "the player can fail, bleed, and lose" and nothing behind it can produce a cost. The check vocabulary has success and failure only. The remedy the seats agree on is a cost vocabulary: a third outcome (it works, and something is spent), the non-HP costs named as consequence tools rather than bookkeeping, an unscathed-fight nudge on the register-note shape, and a sentence that withdrawal is a real ending. No dice change, no difficulty change, no touch on the death walk.

The engine owns instruments for pressure and never starts them

Harper 3 · Baker 8 · Laws 2, 7
The campaign clock is a better progress clock than a drawn circle and in ninety turns the GM set zero new schedules or future events; the world is continuous in memory and static in initiative. Twenty-two consecutive fresh-campaign turns passed with no up beat and no down beat while every drift guard read green. The seats want observers, not rules: a threat clock asked for when prose states a deadline-shaped danger, an off-screen-badness note naming an existing NPC, a beat-shape observer beside the stale-quest nudge, and the wildcard offered on occasions rather than a seven-turn metronome.

The buttons are the story's shape, and they are measured on nothing

Laws 1, 3, 4 · Baker 3, 5 · Moran 4 · Le Guin 1
The three suggestions plus the engine's fourth are the one piece of prose the player is guaranteed to read. Verified against game.js: the fourth button's rest branch fires on a bare HP ratio and topped the ladder while the hero lay unconscious in a cell; the consumable branch has no memory of its own refused offer and outranks the montage. Of 150 fresh-campaign suggestions, 93 were procedural and none touched a relationship; one mature set repeated verbatim three turn-pairs running. The fixes are small: a one-turn refusal memory and scene-safety gate on the fourth button, a divergence requirement (differ in approach or risk; one option that withdraws, waits or pays) and a dramatic kind in the suggestion ask, and a read-only shape census on the drift-health readout.

Prose: the bans work and the shapes do not

Abercrombie 5, 6 · Le Guin 2, 3, 8 · Leiber 8 · Jemisin 5
The register guard is the only closed loop between STYLE and output, and it caught a real slip at turn 2,102. Every other prose rule has the prompt half and not the loop half: sonnet averaged 32 words a sentence against a one-image-per-sentence clause that gemini honoured at 19.6; the antiquity ban was avoided as a word and kept as a gesture ("a mountain's ancient patience"); a simile vehicle recurred, "iron" landed twenty times in twenty turns, and the engine narrated itself in prose twice ("The engine flags nothing to charge"). The seats agree on the pattern of record: scanner first, ring second, one-shot note third — for sentence shape, rhetorical repeats and engine-speak. And the dials should be rewritten as checkable behaviours under #104, because the leguin dial's "timeless tone" contradicts the STYLE tail nine lines later.

People with reasons: the world has memory but no initiative

Baker 4 · Abercrombie 4, 8 · Leiber 5 · Jemisin 3
Companions carry wants by careful design; everyone else carries a mood. Antagonists have hit points and a plot-armour budget and no line in the rules asking for a reason; the fresh campaign's villain speaks thirteen lines and wants nothing on the page. Companions endorse every plan (no contradiction in forty mature turns) because a want is rare by ruling and nothing tells the GM that trait, flaw and motivation govern how an order is taken. A slaver campaign ran fifty turns without an enslaved person speaking. "Banter" appears in the engine twice, both times forbidden. Each fix is one clause, prompt-only, Fable-tier; none touches the want ruling.

Quiet time, and what the engine does with it

Moran 1, 2, 3, 6 · Harper 8 · Le Guin 6
The Moran and Harper lenses disagree productively. The montage detects a pastoral stretch precisely and concludes it should be skipped; Harper wants it cheaper to reach on the downtime signature, Moran wants a "stay in it" sibling. Both are right: quiet time needs two exits. Beneath that: defining moments fire on threshold crossings and the player has no vote (a one-tap "that mattered", engine-composed, is the review's best value-per-byte import); an act closes on XP and records nothing about the person; and the leaping instruction measurably works while the UI renders a sixteen-word beat exactly like a two-hundred-word one.

What the panel would take from this game

Each lens was asked what it would carry away. The list below is what more than one seat named, or one seat named with evidence.

  • The whisper ring — rumour that may exaggerate, misattribute and garble a name but may never invent an event, filed on a ring and served back as hearsay beside the record (Leiber, Jemisin, Laws).
  • The downed ladder and Death's one question — out of action rather than dead, two player moves, a fiction-supplied resolution, a permanent mark, and a mandatory-canon answer at the true death; the pit fight at t46–47 is the recipe executed (Harper, Baker, Abercrombie, Moran).
  • The engine-note registry — a closed, ordered, contract-tested move list with a declared shape per entry; the MC sheet made enforceable, and the "new channel" lesson about authority and recency (Baker, Jemisin).
  • The naming invariant — "the same name IS the same entity, permanently," with alias and merge as its rites; Earthsea's law arrived at from the engineering side (Le Guin).
  • Wants never manufactured, defining moments witness-copied, levels landing at camp — three shipped rulings the Moran lens says are stronger versions of its own positions (Moran).
  • The leaping instruction, with proof — 137 words down to 16 as the world stops changing, back up when it moves (Le Guin).
  • The disclaim discipline and the GM-eyes-only fence — four places where the engine refuses to decide for the player, and a skeleton the world does not know (Baker, Laws, Harper, Jemisin).
  • The register guard — the only prose rule with a scanner and a correcting note behind it, and it caught a frontier model at turn 2,102 (Abercrombie, Le Guin, Leiber).
